## Metacognitive Calibration

*(Subsection within "How Interview Findings Shaped Decisions," after "Tailored Resource Recommendations")*

In addition to capturing what students understood, confidence ratings collected alongside the WI responses provide a window into the degree to which students are aware of the accuracy of their own thinking. Because the WI includes a paired confidence rating for each item on a 1 to 5 Likert scale, the dashboard is able to surface this information by displaying average confidence alongside actual performance rates across Anchoring Concepts. This pairing allows instructors to move beyond a purely accuracy-based interpretation of their students' data and consider the metacognitive dimension of the response patterns they observe.

A pattern that emerged across the interview study was instructors' tendency to draw interpretive conclusions about student understanding that extended beyond what the response data alone could support. In some cases, instructors made inferences about what students were "thinking" or "intending" that relied more on existing assumptions about student engagement than on direct evidence from the data. {[Supporting quote from interview data here — see note about Bob]}. Confidence data offers a more grounded basis for those interpretations. When students respond incorrectly with high confidence, that pattern signals something qualitatively different from a low-confidence incorrect response: overconfident alternate conceptions are more resistant to change because students have less internal motivation to seek out or accept corrective information.

The calibration map at the center of this view displays each Anchoring Concept as a point in a confidence-by-performance space, organized into four interpretive quadrants: well-calibrated, overconfident, underconfident, and low engagement. A corresponding calibration gap chart quantifies the difference between students' average confidence and their expected performance score, making priority areas immediately visible. Anchoring Concepts where students are simultaneously overconfident and hold prominent alternate conceptions represent the highest instructional priority, because students in those areas are least likely to recognize a need to update their thinking and where instructor-initiated scaffolding may have the most impact. This view complements the Anchoring Concept Trends page by adding a metacognitive layer to what would otherwise be a purely accuracy-based data representation.

## Technical Implementation

*(Standalone subsection at the same level as "Overview of Application")*

The Water Instrument Dashboard is a multi-page web application built using Python and the Streamlit framework, which enables development of interactive, browser-accessible data applications without specialized infrastructure. Interactive data visualizations are rendered using Plotly, and data processing relies on standard scientific Python libraries. The full application source code is hosted on GitHub.

Data entry for the tool follows a structured Excel format in which each row represents one student and columns encode item responses (Q1 through Q38, as integer answer choices 1 through 4) and accompanying confidence ratings (QA1 through QA38, on a 1 to 5 Likert scale). Instructor data files may be stored either locally or via a cloud storage link, and the authentication system associates each user account with their own dataset so that a single deployed instance can serve multiple instructors without requiring data sharing or direct interaction with raw files. When no data file is configured, the application generates a realistic simulated dataset that allows instructors to explore the interface before administering the instrument.

The application is organized around five views accessible from a persistent sidebar: a landing page with orientation text, the Predicted vs. Actual radar comparison, the Anchoring Concept Trends page, the Student Confidence page, and a placeholder for future demographic analysis. Each view is designed to be entered in any order after the initial prediction exercise, though the Predicted vs. Actual view is intentionally placed first in navigation so instructors encounter it before seeing outcome data. The application is deployed via Streamlit Community Cloud. To support replication of this design approach for other assessment instruments, the codebase is structured so that item content, Anchoring Concept groupings, and tailored resource mappings are centralized in a single data module that can be replaced to target a different instrument with minimal changes to the interface layer.

## Implications and Future Directions

The Water Instrument Dashboard represents a concrete attempt to operationalize the full Data-Driven Inquiry cycle for instructors who are not researchers. The prior study on which the design is grounded found that instructors could generally move from evidence to conclusion, but consistently stalled at the transition from conclusion to action (Koevoets-Beach et al., in press). By embedding resources, flagging, and asset-oriented framing directly into the data display, the tool is designed to scaffold each step of that cycle within a single interface: the Anchoring Concept Trends page supports evidence-based sensemaking, prominent AC flagging supports focused conclusion-making, and the tailored resource recommendations support the move to specific instructional action. The Predicted vs. Actual view adds a metacognitive layer for the instructor, prompting the kind of reflective calibration that the prior study found to be a productive precursor to revisionary thinking. Together, these features attempt to reduce the interpretive labor that currently falls on instructors when they encounter assessment data without institutional support.

The design process documented here also demonstrates a broader possibility for closing the researcher-practitioner gap in chemistry education. Assessment instruments are typically developed to serve research purposes, with instructors positioned as data providers rather than primary beneficiaries of the knowledge generated. By using interview data to empirically derive what instructors need in order to act on instrument results, the design process treated instructor engagement as a design input rather than an afterthought. The result is a tool whose features can each be traced back to a documented pattern in how instructors actually behave with assessment evidence. This kind of evidence-grounded design, in which qualitative interview findings serve as functional requirements, offers a model that instrument developers could apply regardless of the specific instrument or domain.

Several limitations of the current version warrant acknowledgment. The dashboard is designed specifically for the Water Instrument and is not yet transferable to other assessment instruments without modifying the underlying codebase, which requires developer involvement. Its content and framing are grounded in general chemistry contexts, and applicability to other course levels or disciplines would require validation. The tailored pedagogical resource library, while grounded in the CER literature, has not yet been empirically tested for its effect on instructor behavior or student outcomes. The AC prominence threshold used to flag items is also sensitive to sample size: in smaller cohorts, response patterns may not be stable enough for the flagging logic to be reliably informative, and instructors should interpret flagged items with that constraint in mind.

Future directions for this work include usability studies with instructors across institutional contexts to evaluate whether the tool's design assumptions hold across different pedagogical backgrounds and course structures. Iterative refinement based on structured instructor feedback will be needed to assess whether the framing of resources and visualizations is consistently interpreted as intended. Research investigating whether tool use shifts the framing or action-type patterns that instructors produce when engaging with assessment data would provide direct evidence of impact. Finally, because the application codebase is designed with modular content separation, there is potential to generalize this approach to other instruments by sharing the architecture with other research groups developing assessment-based instructor supports. Making this infrastructure publicly available could accelerate development of similar tools for other assessment instruments across chemistry and beyond.
